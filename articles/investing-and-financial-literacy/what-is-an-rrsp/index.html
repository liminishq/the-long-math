What Is an RRSP? Registered Retirement Savings Plan Explained (Canada)

9 minute read

Quick Answer

An RRSP (Registered Retirement Savings Plan) is a Canadian tax-advantaged investment account that lets you deduct contributions from your taxable income today, grow investments without annual taxation, and defer tax until withdrawal — ideally in retirement, when your tax rate is lower. It is one of the most powerful wealth-building tools available to Canadians because it produces two compounding advantages at once: an immediate tax refund and decades of tax-sheltered growth.

RRSP Key Takeaways

  • Contributions reduce your taxable income immediately — you get a refund (or owe less) when you file.
  • Investments inside the account grow without annual taxation.
  • Withdrawals are taxed as income — the strategy depends on withdrawing when your tax rate is lower.
  • Your contribution limit is 18% of last year’s earned income, up to the annual government maximum ($32,490 for 2025).
  • Unused contribution room carries forward indefinitely.
  • RRSPs must be converted to a RRIF or annuity by December 31 of the year you turn 71.

What Is an RRSP?

An RRSP is not a specific investment. It is a registered, tax-advantaged account — a container the Canadian government places around your investments so the CRA taxes them differently than it would in a regular account.

Inside an RRSP you can hold stocks, ETFs, bonds, GICs, mutual funds, and cash — the same assets available in any brokerage account.

What changes is not what you hold.
What changes is how and when the government taxes the money.

The RRSP follows a simple sequence:

Your Income
↓
RRSP Contribution → Tax Deduction → Refund
↓
Tax-Sheltered Growth (no annual tax drag)
↓
Withdrawal in Retirement
↓
Taxed as Income (ideally at a lower rate)

That timing difference — pay less tax now, pay some tax later — is where the RRSP’s advantage lives.

The Core Idea: Tax Rate Arbitrage

The RRSP is fundamentally a tax-rate timing strategy. You contribute when your tax rate is high and withdraw when your tax rate is lower. The wider that gap, the bigger the permanent advantage.

Inspectable Logic — RRSP Core Benefit

RRSP benefit ≈ contribution tax rate − withdrawal tax rate

Example:

Contribution tax rate: 40%
Withdrawal tax rate: 25%
Permanent advantage: 15 percentage points per dollar contributed

If your retirement tax rate ends up equal to or higher than today, the advantage shrinks or reverses. This is why the comparison with your TFSA matters.

Who Can Open an RRSP?

Any Canadian resident with earned income and a filed tax return can open an RRSP.

Earned income includes:

  • employment income
  • self-employment income
  • certain rental income

Investment income alone does not generate RRSP contribution room.

You can contribute to your own RRSP until December 31 of the year you turn 71.

After that, the account must convert to:

  • a RRIF
  • an annuity
  • or be withdrawn.

You can still contribute to a spousal RRSP if your spouse is younger than 71.

Why the RRSP Matters: The Math

Most explanations of the RRSP stop at the tax deduction.
The real advantage becomes obvious only when you run the arithmetic.

The RRSP works on two levels simultaneously:

  • It reduces your tax bill today.
  • It removes the drag of annual taxation from your investments for decades.

Each effect is meaningful on its own. Together, they compound.

The Immediate Tax Deduction

When you contribute to an RRSP, that amount is deducted from your taxable income for the year.

Inspectable Calculation — The Refund

Tax refund = contribution × marginal tax rate

Example:

Contribution: $15,000
Tax rate: 40%
Refund: $6,000

That $6,000 would otherwise go to the CRA. Instead, it stays in your portfolio.

What you do with it determines how much of the RRSP advantage you actually capture.

If the refund is reinvested — ideally into a TFSA — the compounding effect multiplies.
If it is spent, much of the advantage disappears.

Not sure of your marginal tax rate? The Canada Personal Income Tax Calculator can estimate it based on your income and province.

The Compounding Advantage

Outside a registered account, taxes reduce your effective return every year.

Interest income is taxed annually.
Dividends are taxed annually.
Capital gains allow deferral, but are still eventually taxed.

Inside an RRSP, none of that applies.

Inspectable Logic — Tax Drag

After-tax return = nominal return × (1 − annual tax rate)

Example:

Nominal return: 7%
Tax rate: 40%
After-tax return: 7% × (1 − 0.40) = 4.2%

Over 30 years, 4.2% compounding versus 7% compounding produces an enormous difference in outcome. The arithmetic of how compounding rates diverge over long time horizons is explored in Compound Interest: The Ultimate Wealth Builder. A Common RRSP Mistake

Some investors treat the RRSP tax refund as a bonus and spend it.

When the refund is not reinvested, a large portion of the RRSP’s mathematical advantage disappears. The refund is not extra money — it is part of the account’s structure. Reinvesting it, ideally into a TFSA or additional RRSP room, is what allows the strategy to compound properly.

One Deposit, Three Outcomes

Assume:

  • $15,000 invested once
  • 7% annual return
  • 30 years
  • 40% marginal tax rate today
  • 25% tax rate in retirement

Inside an RRSP

$15,000 × (1.07)^30 = $114,184
Withdrawal tax (25%) = − $28,546
After-tax value = $85,638

Taxable account — interest income

Interest taxed annually at 40%.

$15,000 × (1.042)^30 = $52,002

Taxable account — capital gains

$15,000 × (1.07)^30 = $114,184
Tax on gain ($99,184) at 20% = − $19,837
After-tax value = $94,347

At first glance the capital gains scenario appears to outperform the RRSP. But that comparison overstates the taxable account in two ways.

First, it assumes the entire portfolio generates pure capital gains.
Second, it ignores the starting dollar problem.

Inspectable Logic — The Pre-Tax Dollar Comparison

RRSP contributions use pre-tax dollars.
Taxable investments use after-tax dollars.

Pre-tax income available: $15,000
After-tax capital (40% rate): $9,000

A fair comparison must equalize the starting dollars.

The Full Strategy: Reinvest the Refund

The refund is not a bonus. It is part of the math.

Refund of $6,000 invested at 7% for 30 years:

$6,000 × (1.07)^30 = $45,674

Total wealth created:

RRSP after-tax value: $85,638
TFSA from refund: + $45,674

Total = $131,312

Taxable capital-gains scenario:

$94,347

Same income. Same return. Same time horizon.
The difference is the account structure — and whether the refund was reinvested.

When an RRSP Is Most Powerful

  • High income today, lower income in retirement
    The larger the tax-rate gap between contribution and withdrawal, the larger the advantage.
  • The refund is reinvested
    A major portion of the RRSP’s long-term advantage comes from reinvesting the refund.
  • Interest-generating investments
    Interest income is taxed at your full marginal rate annually outside registered accounts. Shelter it inside an RRSP and that drag disappears.
  • US dividend-paying stocks
    The Canada–US tax treaty exempts RRSPs from the 15% US withholding tax on dividends.
  • Spousal RRSPs
    Spousal contributions allow income splitting in retirement.

RRSP Withdrawals: How the Tax Works

RRSP withdrawals are added to your taxable income for the year and taxed at your marginal rate.

Financial institutions withhold tax at source:

  • 10% under $5,001
  • 20% up to $15,000
  • 30% above $15,000

(rates differ in Quebec)

This withholding is a prepayment, not the final tax bill.

The Two Special Exceptions

Home Buyers’ Plan (HBP)
Up to $60,000 withdrawn for a first home. Repaid over 15 years. Canadians buying their first home should also consider the FHSA, which combines RRSP-style deductions with TFSA-style tax-free withdrawals for a first home purchase.

Lifelong Learning Plan (LLP)
Up to $20,000 withdrawn for education. Repaid over 10 years.

Both programs are structured borrowing from retirement savings.

Inspectable Logic — Which Account to Use First

RRSP usually better when:
current tax rate > retirement tax rate

TFSA usually better when:
current tax rate ≤ retirement tax rate

Most Canadians benefit from using both accounts together.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an RRSP in simple terms?

An RRSP is a Canadian investment account where contributions reduce taxable income today, investments grow tax-sheltered, and withdrawals are taxed later.

Is an RRSP better than a TFSA?

RRSPs work best when your current tax rate is higher than your retirement tax rate.

Should high-income earners prioritize RRSPs?

Often, yes. High-income earners receive larger tax deductions because RRSP contributions are deducted at their marginal tax rate. If retirement income falls into a lower tax bracket, the difference between the contribution tax rate and withdrawal tax rate creates a permanent advantage.

The Bottom Line

The RRSP does not change how markets behave. Returns are what they are.

What the RRSP changes is how much of those returns you keep — by removing the annual tax drag on growth and shifting income from high-tax years to lower-tax years.

The refund is not a side benefit. It is part of the strategy. Canadians who reinvest their RRSP refund — rather than spending it — consistently come out far ahead.

Used with intention — and with the refund reinvested — the RRSP becomes one of the most powerful wealth-building tools available to Canadians.
