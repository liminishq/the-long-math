RRSP Deduction Timing Calculator

This page documents the formulas, assumptions, and shared engines used to compare claiming an RRSP deduction now with saving it for a future higher-income year.

No opinions. No hidden assumptions. Just arithmetic.

Back to Calculator

1. What the calculator answers

The calculator compares two uses of the same RRSP deduction amount:

  • Claim now: claim the RRSP deduction against current employment income, receive the current-year tax saving, then compound that refund for the waiting period.
  • Claim later: do not claim the deduction now, then claim the same deduction against expected future employment income.

The output is the difference between those two values:

Deferral Advantage = Future Tax Saving − Future Value of Current Refund

A positive result means waiting is ahead in the model. A negative result means claiming now is ahead.

2. Inputs

  • Tax year: the Canadian federal and provincial or territorial tax data set used for both current and future comparisons.
  • Province or territory: residence for provincial or territorial tax purposes.
  • RRSP deduction amount: the contribution amount being compared for deduction timing.
  • Current employment income: employment income before claiming the RRSP deduction now.
  • Expected future employment income: employment income in the year when the deduction would be claimed later.
  • Years until future income: the number of years the current refund could be invested or used against debt.
  • Annual return or debt rate: the nominal annual rate applied to the current refund.

3. Shared tax engine

The calculator uses the existing Canadian personal income tax engine:

calculators/canada-income-tax/js/tax.engine.js
computePersonalTax(input)

Before calculating, it loads the selected year’s tax data through:

calculators/canada-income-tax/js/tax.data.js
loadTaxData(year, { basePath: "/calculators/canada-income-tax/data" })

The tax engine includes federal and provincial or territorial brackets, non-refundable credits, employment income, CPP, EI, RRSP deductions, FHSA deductions, dividends, capital gains, and other supported fields. This calculator intentionally uses the employment-income path only for the first draft.

4. Current-year tax saving

The current-year tax saving is calculated by running the tax engine twice:

Current Tax Before = tax(current employment income, RRSP deduction = 0)
Current Tax After  = tax(current employment income, RRSP deduction = deduction amount)

Current Tax Saving = Current Tax Before − Current Tax After

The calculator uses federal plus provincial or territorial income tax. CPP and EI are not part of the refund comparison because an RRSP deduction does not reduce payroll contributions.

5. Future-year tax saving

The future-year tax saving uses the same tax-year rules selected by the user:

Future Tax Before = tax(future employment income, RRSP deduction = 0)
Future Tax After  = tax(future employment income, RRSP deduction = deduction amount)

Future Tax Saving = Future Tax Before − Future Tax After

This means the calculator does not forecast future tax legislation. It asks: using today’s known tax data, how large would the income jump need to be for waiting to beat claiming now?

6. Refund opportunity cost

If the deduction is claimed now, the current tax saving is available immediately. The calculator compounds that amount for the waiting period using the shared investment growth engine:

assets/js/investment-growth.engine.js
InvestmentGrowthEngine.simulateInvestment({
  startingAmount: Current Tax Saving,
  contributionPerPeriod: 0,
  years: Years To Wait,
  nominalAnnualReturn: Annual Rate,
  inflationAnnual: 0
})

The same formula is used for either investment return or debt payoff rate:

Future Value of Current Refund = Current Tax Saving × (1 + annual rate)^years

7. Blended deduction rates

The calculator reports blended deduction rates instead of relying only on the top marginal rate:

Current Blended Rate = Current Tax Saving ÷ Deduction Amount
Future Blended Rate  = Future Tax Saving ÷ Deduction Amount

This matters because a large RRSP deduction can cross more than one federal or provincial bracket. The blended rate is the actual tax saved per dollar of the deduction under the model.

8. Break-even formulas

The future tax saving must at least equal the future value of the refund available today:

Required Future Tax Saving = Future Value of Current Refund

Converted to a blended deduction rate:

Required Future Blended Rate =
  Required Future Tax Saving ÷ Deduction Amount

The break-even annual return on today’s refund is:

Break-Even Annual Return =
  (Future Tax Saving ÷ Current Tax Saving)^(1 ÷ years) − 1

If the actual investment return or debt payoff rate is higher than this break-even rate, claiming now wins. If it is lower, waiting may win.

9. Assumptions and limitations

  • Employment income only in the first draft.
  • No Canada Child Benefit, GST/HST credit, OAS recovery tax, GIS, tuition credits, medical credits, spousal credits, or other benefit interactions.
  • No alternative minimum tax modelling.
  • RRSP contribution room is not calculated here. Use the RRSP contribution room and tax refund calculator for room and deduction-limit estimates.
  • Future income is evaluated using the selected year’s tax data, not forecast legislation.
  • Inflation is not separately modelled in the first draft. The opportunity-cost rate is nominal.
